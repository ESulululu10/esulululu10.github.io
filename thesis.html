Authoring motion for robot teams

My M.S. thesis work: people without robotics expertise author the motions themselves, a model composes those motions for a new scenario, and nobody moves a robot until the person has approved the plan.

Status
Full paper under review, HRI 2027
Role
System design, implementation, study design and analysis
Where
PLAYLab, University of Michigan Flint
Evaluation
Controlled within subjects user study

This page describes the work in general terms while the paper is under review. The system name, the architecture and results figures, and video of the fleet are held back until the review process allows them to be posted. My CV carries the full detail, and I am glad to send figures and a demo to faculty who ask — email me.

The problem

Expressive robot motion works: a robot that moves the right way can tell a bystander what it is doing without a screen or a voice. But in the systems I had built and read about, those behaviors were hand engineered for one scenario. Change the task — a different room, a different set of robots, a different job — and a researcher has to go back and author the motion again. That is a bottleneck no end user can get past, and it is exactly what my earlier system, ExpressBot, ran into: the behaviors were legible, and they were stuck.

My thesis starts from the opposite end. The person authors the motions, in a way that needs no robotics background, and the system’s job is to reuse what they authored when the scenario changes.

The stance

A language model is good at reading “three robots search a room; one signals urgently when it finds something” and proposing who should do what. It is not something I want quietly driving hardware in a room with people in it. So the work is built around a division of labor:

That last stage is not a safety afterthought bolted on at the end — it is the part I am actually studying. What can a person tell about a plan a model proposed? What do they change, and what do they let through? Those questions are what make this an HRI problem rather than a robotics one.

What I built

  • Two ways to author a motion, neither of which assumes a robotics background: one where the person sketches the path they want, and one where they demonstrate the motion physically. Those are the two conditions in the study — the same authoring task, done through a screen or through the body.
  • A planning stage that turns a scenario written in plain English into per robot assignments drawn from the user’s own motions, and that refuses and regenerates output which does not hold up structurally, rather than passing it to the robots.
  • The robot fleet and its tracking — a multi robot platform with a central control server and motion capture grade localization. That stack is itself a finding: the first localization approach I built on was not accurate enough to tell whether a robot had actually performed the motion the user authored, so I replaced it.

The study

I designed and ran a counterbalanced, within subjects study comparing the two authoring methods, combining think aloud protocol during authoring with standard workload and usability instruments afterwards and semi structured interviews at the end — so that what people said while authoring can be read against what they reported once they were done. Participant counts, measures and results are in my CV and in the paper.

My role: I built this end to end. I identified the reuse limitation in prior work and designed the system around it, then built all of it — the robots and their electronics, the localization and the control server that drives them, both authoring interfaces, and the planning and validation layer behind them. The interface work was as much of the problem as the hardware: it has to be usable by someone who has never programmed a robot, in one sitting, under observation. I then designed, ran and analyzed the user study. Carried out in PLAYLab.
